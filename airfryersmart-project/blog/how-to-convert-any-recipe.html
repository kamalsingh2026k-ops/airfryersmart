How to Convert Any Oven Recipe to an Air Fryer

2026-08-16 · 9 min read · AirFryerSmart editorial team

Converting an oven recipe to an air fryer is not guesswork, but it is also not a single magic rule. There is a reliable method: start from the standard rule, adjust for what the food is made of, adjust for your specific machine, adjust for how much you are cooking, and verify with a thermometer. Do that every time and you will get consistent results.

The base rule: 25°F and 20% less

An air fryer is a small convection oven. Its fan moves hot air directly across the food surface, which strips away the insulating layer of humid air that slows browning in a conventional oven. Because heat reaches the surface faster, you need less of it.

The standard conversion: reduce the oven temperature by 25°F (about 15°C) and cut the cooking time by 20 to 25%.

A recipe calling for 400°F for 30 minutes becomes roughly 375°F for 23 minutes. Start checking two minutes before the converted time the first time you make something — every machine differs slightly, and it is far easier to add a minute than to un-crisp something.

Step 1: adjust for food type

The base rule assumes an average food. Real food does not care about averages:

  • Baked goods and desserts: drop the temperature much further — about 40°F below the oven setting. The fan browns tops aggressively; at ordinary baking temperatures, cookies and cakes colour on the outside while the middle is still raw. This is the most common conversion failure.
  • Frozen convenience foods (fries, nuggets, breaded anything): drop only about 15°F. These are par-fried and designed to be re-crisped — they need the heat kept high.
  • Dense items (potatoes, whole chicken, casseroles): the temperature drop stays around 25°F, but be patient with the time. The inside of a dense food takes as long to cook as it always did; the air fryer only speeds up the surface.
  • Delicate seafood: a smaller drop (about 20°F) and a shorter time factor — shrimp can go from perfect to rubber in under 90 seconds.
  • Reheating leftovers: this is not conversion at all. Reheating only needs to reach 165°F (74°C) throughout — roughly 3-4 minutes at 350°F for most things.

Step 2: adjust for your air fryer model

Basket-style air fryers (Ninja, Philips, Cosori, Instant Vortex and similar) have a small chamber with the fan close above the food, so they run effectively hotter than the dial suggests. Oven-style models and air-fryer toaster ovens have a much larger cavity, weaker airflow and more thermal mass — they need roughly 10°F more and about 10% more time.

Within basket units there are still differences. Some models run hot by a few degrees, others run cool. It is worth running one reference cook when you first get a machine: a batch of frozen fries or a single chicken breast, and noting how the results compare to the table. After that, your personal calibration is a one-time mental note.

Step 3: adjust for how much food

Crowding is the silent killer of air fryer results. The basket cooks by moving air around each piece of food, and every extra piece blocks someone else's airflow.

  • A comfortable single layer: baseline, no adjustment.
  • Large batch (about 2 lb): add about 13% to the time.
  • Family size (3 lb and up): add about 25%, and expect softer results — two batches will always be crispier than one full basket.
  • Very light load: knock a few percent off; a nearly empty basket cooks fast.

If a recipe says "spread in a single layer" and your basket is fuller than that, you are in batch territory. Respect the single layer — it is doing more work than the temperature.

Step 4: adjust for frozen

Frozen food needs roughly 35% more time, which in practice is 5-15 extra minutes depending on the length of the cook. The classic mistakes are the opposite of intuition: never thaw breaded or par-fried food first. Thawing releases moisture into the coating, which then steams instead of crisping. Frozen nuggets, fries and fish sticks go straight from the freezer to the basket, no oil, no thawing.

Plain frozen protein — chicken breast, salmon, shrimp — also cooks from frozen in the air fryer, but use a lower temperature (about 15-25°F below the fresh setting) so the centre catches up before the outside dries out. Our food database lists fresh and frozen settings separately for 51 foods.

When conversion does not work

Some things genuinely do not convert, and knowing this saves you a ruined dinner:

  • Wet batters (beer batter, tempura): the batter drips through the basket before it sets. Use a dry breading — flour, egg, panko — instead.
  • Anything that must fit a large flat surface: two dozen cookies, a full sheet-pan dinner. Batch them or use the oven.
  • Delicate custards and cheesecakes: moving air sets the surface before the interior firms.
  • Anything taller than your basket: if the food is within an inch of the heating element, the top burns while the middle stays raw.
  • Empty parchment paper in a preheating air fryer: the fan can lift it into the element. Always weigh it down with food.

The bottom line

Start at 25°F lower and 20-25% less time, then apply four small corrections: food type, your machine, batch size and frozen state. Buy a thermometer and use it. That sequence will convert literally any recipe, and the margin for error is far smaller than people expect — the air fryer is genuinely forgiving once you stop overcrowding it.
