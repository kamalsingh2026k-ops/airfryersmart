How to Clean an Air Fryer Properly (and What Ruins It)

2026-08-16 · 7 min read · AirFryerSmart editorial team

Air fryers are genuinely easy to maintain — easier than an oven, and far easier than a deep fryer. But "easy" is not "automatic". The mistake most owners make is cleaning only the basket and ignoring the rest of the machine, which is where the problems actually live.

Why air fryers smoke (it is not the basket)

When an air fryer starts smoking mid-cook, the cause is almost always grease on the heating element — the exposed coil or plate at the top of the chamber. Hot air circulates, vaporizing that old grease, and the smoke carries into your food. Cleaning the basket does nothing about this because the grease never touched the basket.

The second cause is fat pooling beneath the basket during cooking — rendered chicken or bacon fat hitting the hot element. Two tablespoons of water in the outer drawer fixes most of that on the spot. But the permanent fix is element hygiene.

The three-zone cleaning system

Zone 1 — the basket and drawer (every use). Wash in warm soapy water by hand, or check the manual — some baskets are dishwasher-safe and some are not. Non-stick baskets from brands like Ninja and Cosori generally prefer hand washing; the dishwasher's heat and detergents gradually dull the coating. Dry thoroughly before storing; water trapped in the coating's surface is what breeds the sticky film that appears overnight.

Zone 2 — the heating element and interior (weekly). With the machine unplugged and completely cool, wipe the element and the chamber walls with a damp cloth. For stubborn spots, a paste of baking soda and water on a soft cloth, then a clean damp wipe. Never use metal scourers or abrasive pads anywhere near the element — the coils are fragile and the reflective surface matters for even heating.

Zone 3 — the exterior and vents (monthly, or when you see dust). Wipe the outside with a damp cloth. Check the intake and exhaust vents on the back or bottom — they collect dust, and a blocked vent both slows cooking and makes the machine work hotter than it should.

What actually damages the non-stick coating

Four things kill a non-stick basket, in rough order of frequency:

  • Aerosol cooking sprays. The propellants and emulsifiers break down the coating over months of use. Use a pump mister or a brush instead. This is the single most common cause of "my basket got sticky" posts.
  • Metal utensils. Metal tongs, forks and spatulas score the coating. Silicone or wooden tools only.
  • Dishwashers on non-dishwasher-safe models. High heat and alkaline detergents age the coating fast. Read the manual — if it says hand wash, it means it.
  • Cleaning while hot. Thermal shock warps and cracks the coating. Always let the machine fully cool before washing.

Once the coating is gone it cannot be restored — no amount of re-seasoning helps, and new baskets cost more than the care would have.

Baked-on grease: the method that works

Soak the basket in hot water with a few drops of dish soap for 15-20 minutes — the grease softens and slides off with a sponge, no scraping required. For genuinely stubborn carbon spots, make a baking soda and water paste, apply it, leave 10 minutes, then wipe. If your model's basket has a removable base plate (many do), remove and clean it separately — that plate hides more grease than people realise.

Never soak a model with a non-removable electrical base, and never submerge the machine body. Only the basket and drawer are ever immersed.

Safety checks worth doing monthly

  • Unplug the machine and inspect the power cord for damage, especially near the plug and where it enters the machine.
  • Check that the heating element has no visible cracks or discoloration.
  • Confirm the fan spins freely and quietly — a grinding noise means crumbs or debris are in the fan housing; unplug and clear it.
  • Make sure the vent areas are clear of dust, and never operate the machine with anything resting on top of it.

How cleaning connects to your cooking

A clean machine is not just hygienic — it cooks better. A grease-coated element transfers heat unevenly, which is why some people report one side of the basket always browning faster than the other. And the off-taste from burning old grease is the exact reason a batch of wings that should be perfect tastes slightly wrong. The food database timings assume a working, clean machine; so does every number in the conversion calculator.

The bottom line

Wash the basket after every use, wipe the element weekly, deep clean monthly, and ban aerosol spray and metal utensils for life. That is the entire system — about twenty minutes a month that keeps a £100 appliance performing like day one and your food tasting like the machine was designed to make it.
